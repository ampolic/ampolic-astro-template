## Heat rises, and your house proves it

It's a familiar summer complaint: the thermostat downstairs reads a comfortable 72, but the upstairs bedrooms feel like an attic. You're not imagining it, and it isn't a broken system. It's physics fighting your ductwork.

Warm air rises. Every summer, heat collects on the upper floor while the cooled air from your AC settles low. Add a roof soaking up sun all day and second-floor windows taking direct light, and the upstairs is fighting a losing battle. The question is what to do about it.

## The easy adjustments

Start with airflow balance. Most homes have adjustable dampers or at least register vanes. Partly closing the registers on the main floor pushes more cooled air upstairs. It feels backward to choke the rooms that are already comfortable, but that's how you rebalance the system.

Next, use the fan. Setting the thermostat fan to "on" keeps air circulating even when the AC isn't cooling, which mixes the layers and evens out the temperature between floors. It costs a little more in fan runtime and saves a lot in comfort.

Ceiling fans help for the same reason. They don't lower the temperature, but moving air makes a room feel three to four degrees cooler, which is often enough to make an upstairs bedroom livable without touching the thermostat.

## The bigger fixes

If the quick adjustments aren't enough, look at the envelope. A hot second floor is often an insulation story: an under-insulated attic lets the roof's heat pour straight down into the bedrooms below. Bringing attic insulation up to current levels is one of the highest-return comfort upgrades in a two-story home, and it cuts winter heat loss too.

Windows matter as well. West- and south-facing upstairs windows take a beating in the afternoon. Cellular shades, exterior awnings, or a low-E film cut the solar gain before it becomes indoor heat.

Then there's the ductwork itself. Builders often run the same duct sizes to both floors, which leaves the upstairs starved because it's fighting gravity for its share of cool air. A technician can measure the airflow and, in some cases, resize or add a run to fix the imbalance at the source.

## When one system isn't enough

For homes that never balance no matter what, zoning is the real answer. A zoned system uses separate thermostats and motorized dampers to cool the upstairs and downstairs independently, so each floor gets what it needs. It's a bigger investment, but for a house that's been uncomfortable for years, it's the fix that finally works.

Most upstairs-is-hotter problems don't need anything that dramatic. Start with the dampers, the fan, and the shades, and see how far that gets you. If the upstairs still bakes, call us and we'll measure the airflow and tell you whether it's a balance issue or something worth zoning.
