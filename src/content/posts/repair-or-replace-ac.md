## The question every August

The AC quits in the middle of a heat wave, the technician quotes a repair, and you're stuck on the same question everyone faces eventually: pour money into a unit on its way out, or bite the bullet and replace it. There's no single right answer, but there is a clear way to think it through.

It comes down to three things: how old the unit is, how much the repair costs, and how well it's been running lately.

## The age and cost test

A useful rule of thumb weighs the repair against the equipment's remaining life. Multiply the repair cost by the age of the unit in years. If that number tops around 5,000, replacement usually makes more sense; if it's well under, the repair is likely worth it.

A $400 repair on a 6-year-old unit comes to 2,400, so you fix it. The same $400 on a 14-year-old unit is 5,600, and you're better off replacing. It's a rough guide, not a law, but it captures the real trade-off: money spent on old equipment buys you less time.

Age alone matters too. Most air conditioners last 12 to 15 years in our climate. Under 10 and a repair usually pays. Past 15 and you're on borrowed time, where each fix buys a season or two before the next failure.

## The signs pointing toward replacement

Beyond the math, a few patterns tip the scale toward a new system. Repeated repairs in a short span mean the unit is unraveling, and the next failure is rarely the last. Rising energy bills from an aging, low-efficiency unit are a cost you pay every month, not just when it breaks.

Then there's the refrigerant. Older systems use R-22, which has been phased out and is now expensive and scarce. A leak in an R-22 system can cost more to recharge than the repair is worth, and that alone often decides it.

Uneven cooling, a unit that struggles to keep up on hot days, and a compressor on its second failure all point the same direction.

## The case for repairing

None of this means replace at the first sign of trouble. A newer unit with a straightforward fault, a bad capacitor, a failed fan motor, a contactor, is almost always worth repairing. These are normal wear items, they're not expensive, and they don't mean the system is failing.

If the unit is under 10 years old, has been reliable, uses current refrigerant, and the repair is modest, fix it and move on. Replacement is a big expense, and there's no reason to rush it on a system with years left.

## Deciding well

The worst outcome is a rushed decision made in a hot house with a pushy quote in hand. When your AC gives you trouble, ask for the repair cost, the unit's age, and an honest read on its condition, then run the numbers.
